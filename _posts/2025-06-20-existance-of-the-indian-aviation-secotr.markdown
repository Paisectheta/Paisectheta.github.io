The crash of AI171 a few weeks back raised some serious concerns over the Indian aviation safety standards. Though lauded, the Indian aviation sector isn’t exactly what we think it to be. The Delhi International and Kempegowda International might be the prettiest terminals in the country - gleaming glass facades, avant-garde architecture, and illusionary order - but beneath the shine lies a struggling, over-stressed industry hanging on by threads.

For starters, safety isn't systemic - it's reactive. It often takes a near-catastrophe or media outcry for regulators to snap into action. Maintenance protocols are stretched thin, with underpaid engineers, ageing fleets, and cut-corner contracts with foreign lessors. There’s little room for long-term risk mitigation when most airlines are fighting for financial survival.

## Beneath the glass facades

And that's the core issue - Indian aviation bleeds cash [1R]. Most airlines operate on margins slimmer than a boarding pass barcode. The fare wars, especially between low-cost giants like IndiGo and SpiceJet (when it’s not faking its own pulse), force unsustainable pricing [1F]. Add to this a ludicrously high tax on aviation fuel (ATF) - up to 30% compared to a global average of ~5% - and you’ve got a recipe for chronic fiscal trauma.

Oversight? The DGCA, while well-intentioned, is bogged down in bureaucracy. Everything - route permissions, aircraft acquisition, even mundane updates - requires navigating a regulatory swamp. Meanwhile, airports in Mumbai and Delhi are bursting at the seams, and tier-2 cities barely have taxiways wide enough for a jet to stretch its wings [2, 3F].

## Financial bleeding and high taxes

We’re pathetically dependent on foreign leasing, with over 85% of Indian aircraft owned abroad, paid for in US dollars. So the moment the rupee wobbles, the whole system shakes. And when one airline defaults (Hello Go First) our credibility takes another nosedive.

Add to that a serious talent drain. Pilots, ATCs, and engineers are overworked, underpaid, and jumping ship to Emirates, Singapore Airlines, and the like. India trains some of the best aviation professionals - only to gift them to countries with better runways and better run regimes.

And what about a national flag-bearer? Air India is still detoxing after decades of bureaucratic bloat. IndiGo is efficient but lacks full-service finesse and international dominance. The rest? Either in ICU or already cremated.

## Bureaucratic oversight and structural leaks

But we’re not done. There’s also:

- **Corruption in procurement:** Shady deals and inflated contracts drain public and private coffers alike.
- **Lack of indigenous aerospace R&D:** HAL, bless its bureaucratic heart, hasn’t produced a serious commercial aircraft worth international notice. For a country that sends missions to the Moon on a shoestring, our domestic aviation tech looks like it's been held together with duct tape and forgotten dreams [4F].

> In truth, Indian aviation is a paradox: five-star terminals wrapped around a three-star system, fuelled by one-star decision-making. Until we fix the fundamentals - tax rationalisation, infrastructure overhaul, indigenous leasing, and actual investment in human and technological capital - we're just flying pretty planes through broken skies.

## The quiet revolution

Yet, for all its flaws, there’s a quiet revolution unfolding.

Passenger growth is among the fastest in the world. With India on the cusp of overtaking the UK to become the third-largest aviation market, demand is roaring. Connectivity is improving, and the UDAN scheme - for all its teething issues - has brought flights to towns that previously never imagined seeing aircraft in their skies [5F].

Air India, now under the Tatas, is beginning to turn around. Ambitious fleet expansions, rebranding, and a push for punctuality are signs of a long-overdue resurrection. IndiGo remains a masterclass in operational efficiency, with enviable load factors and on-time performance that would make many global carriers blush.

## A soaring future

Even infrastructure is inching forward. Jewar Airport, Navi Mumbai, and greenfield projects across the country signal that India's civil aviation ambitions are now being built with concrete, not just committee notes [2R]. The GAGAN navigation system (our own version of GPS-aided air navigation) shows that we can innovate when we set our minds to it [6, 7F].

And let us not forget the passengers themselves - over a billion dreams with wings. India’s flying public is young, aspirational, and unafraid to demand better. They are the pressure and the promise.

So yes, Indian aviation is messy. It’s chaotic, bureaucratic, and frustrating. But it’s also growing, evolving, and - just maybe - learning to soar.
